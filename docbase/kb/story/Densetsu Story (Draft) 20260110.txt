Narrator’s Foreword 
Today Is the Consequence of Yesterday






People say it takes a village to raise a child. In Hirogawa, that meant a hundred small, quiet acts: a bowl refilled without asking, a grip corrected by a passing hand, a name spoken gently when no one else was listening. Love rarely announced itself there—it accumulated. And in that accumulation, without ever meaning to, the village bound Michio to a future he had not chosen.


Sometimes this meant doing chores for other villagers. Gratitude was the usual coin—quiet, sincere, and never demanded back. Yet each thank-you lingered, settling like a gentle claim. Michio wanted to belong to himself, at least as much as he belonged to the village—to everyone in it. It was a question he could not yet name, but one that would occupy his mind and heart for years to come.

Prologue  
Today Is the Consequence of Yesterday

Early Memories


Michio sits on the edge of a waterfall cliff, seen from behind. Dawn breaks slowly. Warm light cuts through cold blue haze as distant hills and mountains pierce the fog. Far below, Hirogawa rests quietly—close enough to protect, far enough to observe.


I climbed to the mountain top to watch the sunrise. I’ve been doing this since I was a child.


Images start forming in his mind. The inner gardens of Ishigane. A koi pond near the gates. Five‑year‑old Michio stands beside his mother, Hitomi. She rests a hand on his head.


My mom took me there all the time. Afterwards we would visit the Daimyo and report any unusual sightings.


Hitomi appears in her prime—binoculars raised, posture alert. A former spy, now a watch‑guardian.


She used to be a spy when the Seigetsu war was heated. Ishigane knew her eyes were sharp enough to keep old war ghosts away from the village.

Foundations


Michio imagines Lord Ishigane in his thirties, serving as a samurai under the Emperor of Seigetsu. 


Cutting Crimson supply lines was crucial for securing what is now Known as Hirogawa. The whole feud, not just the town. He was granted this land not to extend Seigetsu, but to build a neutral nation and keep the Crimsons at bay. Eventually the Crimsons stopped meddling in external affairs because they couldn’t simply reclaim Hirogawa without risking a Seigetsu invasion.


The emperor bestowed honors in person: A land claim, a retirement leave. Ishigane deserved it, he was clever and served well.


More images form in Michio’s mind. Not memories, but a reimagining of another situation he couldn't have witnessed: young Hitomi and Hanzo spying on each other—awkward, playful, human. Almost like a cartoon chase. It culminates in a proud Hanzo beside a pregnant Hitomi.


Mom was a spy. Dad was an investigator. Somehow they ended up snooping on each other… and liking what they found. I guess they wanted out of war. For me.


Michio remembers returning to the Daimyo’s mansion after the morning watch with his mother.  Hitomi, formal as ever, goes down on one knee and bows. Michio doesn't follow.


"Nothing unusual, Ishigane‑dono." reports Hitomi

"Thank you for your service. Proceed with your daily routine." Ishigane replies, stern and emotionally neutral, as usual.
Michio steps between them. 


"Gran’pa Ishi—can Kazuya come play outside?" he naively asks.


Hitomi freezes, embarrassed.


"M‑Michio—! I—I’m so sorry, Ishigane‑dono!" Hitomi apologizes instinctively.


Ishigane pauses… A silence so thick even the best Tamahagane sword couldn't cut through. Then he laughs—a rare, genuine sound.

This was one of the few times I ever saw Ishigane laugh.


Michio leaves the mansion, heading towards the Infinitree. Each step upward is paired with fleeting memories, diffused in water, clouds and in the way light and shadow played tag under the tree canopies.


Clouds formed familiar shapes. Faces appeared in water reflections. Old laughter hid in fields of flowers and patches of greenery.
Roots twisted through stone and earth, climbing skyward. The Infinitree rose higher than the mountain itself—a silent guardian the village seemed to have grown around rather than beneath.


With every jump, every climb, memory followed him.


Five-year-old Michio sat on Hanzo’s shoulders. Lanterns swayed. Music drifted. A festival alive with color and noise. Michio pointed excitedly at something unseen.


"Now that I think about it..." Michio thought, "there weren’t many children like us in the village. Kazuya, myself... We were built differently. Warrior children."


The thought unsettled him.


Children going to war. Carrying the flags of their parents. Killing strangers without knowing their names or their struggles…


I don’t understand war. And thankfully, I’ve never been at one. 
At least not with other people. 


Only with my memories…

Growth


Michio reached the foot of the Infinitree.


From below, its height was overwhelming. Roots like walls. Bark like cliffs. Its immense height is emphasized by the mountain top laying behind the massive roots.


He sees no sign of the Sage and decides to knock on his door. At the base of the tree trunk, where once was a hollow, now there's a wall of stone blocks, laid without much care, but sturdy, unmoving. An arch of the same stone holds a wooden door whose wood has seen far too many seasons - bloated, cracked, moss growing in the fissures.


Three stone steps and an elevated platform. For Michio, a single hop. He knocks slowly.


"Yes? Is there something wrong?" The sage asks behind the closed door.


"Well... um... no... I mean... uh..." Michio was having trouble articulating.


The Sage steps out, closes the door behind him very swiftly, so much so that light barely touched the inside floors.


"I need to gather herbs. Walk with me." he invites. Michio follows, silent.


After walking for a good hour, Michio was able to decompress. The smell of herbs and flowers was like natural healing, the same that the Sage offers the village in balms and teas. He learned some of this herbalist knowledge by proxy rather than interest.


"Look! Lemongrass!" Michio says, proud and excited.
Indeed he was right. The Sage takes his knife out and cuts a batch.
They sit beneath a tree, tea steaming gently between them inside an old kettle on a small campfire. The conversation blurs in smell and taste. 


Later, back in his study, the Sage unrolls a scroll—schematics for a mannequin-like wooden figure connected by a metal armature with movable joints.


"I suppose we all feel lonely and displaced sometimes." Murmurs the Sage to himself, as he prepares the tools and materials.

More frightening than enemies


That evening, Michio returned home. Something wasn’t right…
The lights were on—and yet, no one was home.


“Mom?” Michio called, but only his voice echoed back at him. “Mom?!”


He searched every room. Nothing.


Somehow an empty house felt worse than finding thieves. 
It was eerie. Unfamiliar. Scary… He ran to Ishigane, holding his tears.


The guard stops him. 


"Stop, young man. No visitors without appointment, and besides, shouldn't you be going to bed soon?


"My mom... she's gone!!" Michio cried, tears rolling down his face, making tiny wet spots on the floor as they fell.


"Ishigane-Dono! Emergency! Please advise." The guard announced. 


Ishigane came personally to see what was going on.


"Where is your mother?" Ishigane asks calmly, trying to assess the situation. He knew it's not about inviting Kazuya to play. Michio was visibly disturbed.


"She... She's not home. gone!" Said Michio, sobbing between words.


Ishigane exhaled profusely, not of annoyance, but of disappointment.
"Traders from the Crimson lands came by today." he began “They carried rumors, said Hanzo was seen jumping from a cliff near the Temple of Stillness."


“When pressed, they admitted the story’s been spreading for weeks.” Ishigane said, stern and heavy-hearted.


A pause. A heavy one. Ishigane takes a deep breath.


“Your mother believed none of it,” he continued. “She wanted to see the body. She went alone… and did not return. Our search party found nothing. No sign of struggle. No answers. We were hoping she'd return on her own, but you being here means that didn't happen.”


His voice lowered. He lowered himself to Michio, put his hand on his shoulder, firmly but softly. He looked Michio in the eyes and said:


“I don’t know what to tell you, young man — but stay strong. 
The village is with you.”

The search parties returned without answers. 


Day after day after day… Until they stopped searching. No leads. None.






And that’s how I became an orphan thirteen years ago...


...Through the stubbornness of my parents...

Present of the Past


The sound of water pulled Michio back.


Not the imagined rush of memory, but the real thing—the waterfall behind him, steady and loud. The mist clung to his clothes. His legs ached slightly from sitting too long.


He stood, stretched, and exhaled.


The village below was awake now.
Smoke rose from chimneys. Footsteps crossed wooden bridges. Somewhere, metal clinked against stone. Hirogawa moved as it always did—quietly, purposefully, together.


Michio descended the path with practiced ease. He trained often.
Sometimes under Nagamizu’s watchful eye, correcting his stance with a word or a tap of the staff. Sometimes alone, repeating movements until his muscles burned and his breath came sharp.


Jump. Roll. Recover.


Again. A hundred times. «Too tired to count, but kept going» times.


When training ended, the day simply continued.


He delivered meals from the small restaurant near the square—bowls wrapped in cloth, still warm. The owners always tried to give him extra. He always refused once, then accepted the second time. 
At the farm he carried crates of produce and loaded the wagons for the old couple who ran the fields, especially on market days.
Their backs were bent. His was not.


“Careful now,” they’d say, “or you’ll end up like us!”


“I’ve got it,” he’d answer, already walking, albeit sometimes hardly.


Sometimes he helped replace roof beams. Sometimes he held walls steady while others worked. Sometimes he was just another pair of hands passing tools along.


Each task ended the same way: A nod. A word of thanks. Sometimes a meal or something for later


Every interaction added to the invisible ledger he never meant to keep.
It wasn’t burden. Not exactly. Also not debt. He just had learned to help without expecting reward, and that one hand washes the other.
Together, they wash the face, because the face can’t wash itself.


The hands were the villagers, the face - the village.


And what a beautiful face it is! The fertile farms with endless rows of sprouts looking like a well-groomed beard, the lush greenery reminding of wild, beautiful hair, sometimes curly, sometimes straight, never combed except around the town’s edges. Plowed dirt fields like a skincare routine, and the regular building maintenance was more than beauty salon procedure.
This was no mere facewash, it was an ecosystem of reciprocal care.


But as the sun climbed higher, Michio felt it settle all the same.
It’s a grand machine, no doubt. One that self-sufficiently fuels, oils, and repairs itself, and in the end still has productive output.
A marvel in itself but also by itself. Isolated both literally and figuratively.


The Summon


As Michio enjoys a bowl of Ramen noodles with a runny yolk egg - one of his favorites from the local restaurant - a message reaches him.


Ishigane wished to see him.


Michio wiped his mouth, nodded, and made his way toward the Daimyo’s residence, leaving the meal unfinished. This is important.


Ishigane stood near the open manor gates, looking out over the village. His posture was as straight as ever, but his brow was drawn low.


“You sent for me?” Michio asked.


Ishigane turned.


“The bamboo workers,” he said without preamble. “They did not return last evening. They were due back before dusk.”


A pause. Michio frowns and starts feeling tense like he never felt before.


“Search parties?” Michio asked.


“No sign. No disturbance. No tools left behind.” Ishigane replied.


Michio felt a familiar tightening in his chest.


“How many?” he asks.


“All eight of them.” Ishigane replies in a worried tone.


Silence stretched between them. 
Michio didn’t know what to say, Ishigane studied him carefully.


“You know these forests,” he said. “You know the paths, the shortcuts. You know when something feels wrong.”


Michio met his gaze. “Yes.”


“Then be careful.” Ishigane said while slowly nodding once.


Coming from Ishigane, this was an unspoken order. Nonetheless ,the eerie familiarity of pillars of Michio’s life simply disappearing took him over like a rush. He knew he had to be careful, lest he ends up like them…

A Tragic Romance of Bamboo and Steel
The path sloped downward, away from Hirogawa’s terraces and stone paths, following the river as it widened and slowed. The forest thinned first—old trees giving way to younger growth—until the ground flattened and softened underfoot.


Here, the river no longer cut sharply through rock. It spread.
Water pooled between roots and reeds, trapped in shallow wetlands where the soil stayed dark and fertile year‑round. Bamboo rose in dense clusters, tall and straight, their leaves whispering softly as the wind passed through them.


Stone gave way to soil. The slope softened. Water slowed, pooled, and spread into shallow wetlands where reeds and young bamboo crowded the banks. The air grew heavier here—cool, damp, alive. Insects hummed. Birds watched from the canopy but did not sing.


Bamboo thrived in this place. So did patience.


Michio moved carefully, feet finding dry ground between puddles. He recognized the terrain immediately. This was where Hirogawa’s bamboo was harvested. Fertile soil, fast growth, and easy transport back to the village.


He stopped when the sounds reached him.
Hollow, metallic. Some human, some natural. All a bit unsettling. 


Rustling chains dotted with clanking noises, as if someone was making a pile of metal tubes. Rhythmically and with an almost melodic tone to it,


Voices of strain and exhaustion. Interrupted by jet-like sounds, and a mix of falling bamboo and metallic impacts in a pattern.


From behind a curtain of leaves, Michio saw clearly what was going on:
Four workers were chained to a thick bamboo pole driven into the mud. Their hands were raw. Their movements, mechanical. They worked without pause. They used high-pressure boiled water to cut specific bamboo patches. Steel-veined, gleaming unnaturally. They sound metallic too when they’re being stored in piles…


Hagane bamboo!
Metal-infused. Nearly impervious to melee. An extremely valuable raw material for weaponry, armor and defences. Dangerous too, leaving needle thin metal splinters in the flesh of the inexperienced smith.


The pile beside them was already too large to throw anything on top, menacing to collapse at any moment. They start a new pile next to it.


Michio crouched lower, squinted to focus, as if he was zooming in.


Beyond the workers, movement flickered.
Green-clad figures patrolled the clearing—unarmed, light on their feet. Ninjas. Their posture was relaxed but alert, as if violence were an option they simply hadn’t needed yet.


At the edge of the camp stood their squad leader: a man in a green cap, dark curly hair spilling out from beneath it like black foam. 
He leaned casually against the bamboo pile, chewing on a blade of grass. The bamboo pile screeched, but he was unmoved.


Too casual.
________________


Not in charge—but confident enough to act like he was. He bragged non-stop about how after this mission he would also get promoted and clad in hagane bamboo plates. The other soldiers looked uneasy, as if their martial code had forbidden them to cover their ears.


Michio counted routes. Shadows. Smelled the wind, carrying sweat and particles of clashing materials in the misty air.


A direct fight would get the workers killed.

Cunning Over Force


Michio circled wide, keeping the wilderness sound between himself and the camp. He used the wetlands as cover: mud swallowing footsteps, reeds breaking his silhouette, acting like he was part of this ecosystem.


A ripple where there shouldn’t be one alarmed one of the grunts at the edge of the labor camp, Though it could have been the wildlife around.


One ninja peeled away from the group, moving to investigate.


Michio was already gone, underwater, breathing through a regular bamboo stalk he had carefully prepared beforehand.


He drew them off in pairs. Never leaving the overgrown water-loving flora, never striking. Shadows and noise to unsettle, absence to confuse.


“Don’t chase shadows! Look for the caster!!!” The squad leader barked, irritation creeping into his voice, as the space around Michio filled with sounds of wet footsteps and vegetation being pushed over.


The leader ran in the opposite direction. That was the opening.


Michio slipped back into the clearing while attention fractured. He worked fast with quick, clean cuts. 
Chains loosened. The first worker nearly collapsed.


“Shh.” Michio whispered. “Follow the river. Don’t look back.”


They didn’t argue. One by one, the four workers vanished into the reeds.


The clearing felt suddenly empty. Too empty.
Escalation Toward Midori


Silence stretched. Then voices again. Some known, some new ones.
Another green squad merged in from the treeline, with tighter formation and sharper awareness. They took in the scene at a glance: empty chains, disturbed ground, the absence of workers.


The squad leader straightened.


“Trouble,” he muttered. 


There was no room to disappear. Michio stepped out from the shadows.


Steel rang against bamboo as the first clash broke the stillness. The wetlands churned underfoot. Reeds snapped. Water sprayed.
It was fast. Messy. Inevitable. Red tint would persist until the next rains.


The air felt wrong—like breath that was holding back.


Something else was here.


Not another patrol. Not another mistake.


The wetlands themselves seemed to listen, bamboo leaves freezing mid-whisper as if waiting for permission to move.


Slow clapping echoed from deeper within the bamboo.


“Well done,” a woman’s voice said, calm and amused. “I was wondering how long it would take before someone noticed the chains.”


The bamboo parted. Slowly, she’s not blending in anymore.
She stood there as a hooded figure, fox fur on the outside, green silk on the inside. Short brown hair, tips barely touching her shoulders. Unarmed, emerald eyes, sharp with intent. A subtle but undeniable smell of perfume that begs to dream of blooming summer flower fields.


“General Midori.” She introduced herself, with posture and confidence.
“Good job freeing the workers. Looks like they were actually missed.” 
“Don’t worry, we have more!” she added, smirking


Behind her, deeper within the thicket, Michio glimpsed more structures. More workers. And one figure kept close inside the bamboo patch she came out of. A chain, serpenting back to her hand. A private slave.


Michio tightened his grip on his weapon.
A fight here would cost innocent lives.


And Midori knew it.


The wetlands went quiet. Not peaceful, listening.


Water rippled where Michio stood, bamboo leaves slowly settling from the earlier clash. His breath was steady, controlled. 
He didn’t lower his stance.


That was when the air bent.


It wasn’t visible at first. Just a faint distortion, like heat rising from stone—except the wetlands were cold. 
In Michio’s eye, the bamboo stalks appeared to coil around the location, tightening into a crushing spiral, but somehow their shadows were overlapping where they shouldn’t. His mind started drawing pictures of fire consuming them. He didn’t quite understand but chose to stay calm.


“Is this what being drunk feels like? Michio muttered to himself, as the Illusion slowly vanished, consumed by a fire only his mind could see.


Midor’s sharp laugh echoed through the thicket. Not cruel, genuine.
Measured. Unhurried. And it seemingly delighted the general.


“Well, aren’t you a special one…” Midori said. “Most people panic.”


She stepped forward. The chain she held became tense.
At the end of that chain stood a worker.


Younger than the others. Barely standing. Mind absent from exhaustion.


Michio’s jaw tightened. His stance widens and he shifts his weight down.


Midori tilted her head, studying him like a puzzle with missing parts.


“You freed four,” she said. “Efficient. Quiet. Compassionate… Predictable.”


With the last words, the wetlands shifted.


For a heartbeat, Michio’s footing wasn’t where he expected. Water reflected bamboo that didn’t exist. Shadow armies that tripled their size with each blink of an eye and drew closer when you’re not looking.


Illusions.


Not obstacles — misdirection. 
Once again, consumed by a fire without heat.


Michio adjusted instantly, lowering his center of gravity. 
He didn’t attack - “Let them go,” he said.


Midori laughed softly.


“Oh, dear, you must be really naive.” she replied. Then paused and  followed up with “No wonder you’re the one they sent!”


Michio felt mildly offended. Did she mean they sent him because he’s naive? What could she mean by that? Mind games, perhaps?


She snapped her fingers.


Two more green-clad figures appeared where there had been none a moment before. Whether they were hidden somewhere or an illusion, Michio couldn’t tell.


One lunged.


Michio moved.


He vaulted backward onto a bamboo stalk, using its bend to redirect momentum, springing sideways as a blade passed through the space where his torso had been. His hands connected with the attacker’s wrist—not breaking it, but flinging the attacker using their own motion and weight. The enemy was flung wide with the combined forces.


This felt real.

The second attacker got taken down by the first one flying onto him.


The bamboo piles stuttered.


“You’re good with pairs, let’s see you deal with a Trio.” Midori says.


For half a second, there were three Midoris.


Michio didn’t look at any of them, he watched the hostage. 


Only one hostage. Only one chain. Leading back to the real Midori.


Michio looks her dead in the eyes, frowning his brows.
“Stop joking around!” he said. “Is this a game for you?”


“Interesting…” The real Midori reacted.


She tightened the chain just enough for the worker to gasp.
Michio, already in attack momentum, froze after the first step.


“That hesitation,” Midori said calmly, “that’s your biggest weakness.” 


“And also your greatest strength. You don’t take unmeasured steps.”
She said and followed with a swift gesture.


The remaining troops that had searched for Michio earlier emerge to ambush him where he stands, only a few steps away from Midori.


It all happened too fast to count heads, but there’s about a dozen enemies running and jumping towards Michio at this moment.


Michio didn’t retreat. He leapt up.


Using the bamboo’s flexibility to propel him,he ran along vertical stalks, held to the top of a batch, shifting his weight with impossible precision.


The stalks screeched, bent under Michio’s kinetic force and weight.


When they snapped back, Michio was launched at high speed.


From above, he kicked loose a cluster of Hagane bamboo.


The stalks fell. Not on the guards, between them.
The impact sent a shock through the wet ground, staggering the attackers and breaking their formation.


A tsunami of harvested canes rolled out from the sides of the collapsing batch, almost like a tragic romance of bamboo and steel. 
Some soldiers got buried under canes, others immobilized or neutralized.


Michio ran back, leaping from the highest point of this sea of hagane bamboo, landing beside the chained worker in the same motion.


His blade landed. The chain snapped. 
Midori tries pulling the slave back - only a chain with nothing attached.


Midori’s eyes widened — just slightly.


The freed worker collapsed. Michio caught him, pulling him clear while deflecting a thrown blade with his sword, aimed at where they had been a moment earlier.


“Clever,” Midori said, observant instead of engaging. “Very clever.”


She stepped back.


The wetlands unwound. The lingering illusions in the background peeled away like mist under sunlight. The remaining guards hesitated.


Midori raised a hand.


“Enough.”


She turned her gaze fully to Michio now.


“You fight like someone who thinks they can change the future…
Hold on to that belief. It makes people… interesting.” she said, seriously.


She took another step back. Bamboo shadows swallowed her.


“Tell Ishigane,” her voice echoed, now everywhere and nowhere, 
“that this place has no future. You’ll soon realize by yourself.”


Silence returned.


The guards were gone.


Only the wetlands remained.


Michio steadied the rescued worker, scanning the area one last time.


Midori was gone.


But the fort deeper in the thicket still stood.
And now Michio knew: This wasn’t an accident.


It was the beginning of something

Translating the unspoken


Michio returns to Ishigane’s hall before dusk. 
The space is orderly, quiet. No guards intervene, Michio is expected.
As he walked in he looked at the koi pond. The fish didn’t seem to care about anything. But sometimes they would bite each other.
The aggressive nature of the world was starting to creep into him.


Michio enters the guest house without a word. He kneels. He reports.
Not feelings. Not assumptions. Only what he saw.


Bamboo workers chained. Hagane bamboo stockpiled far beyond civilian needs. Green-clad units operating with discipline. Fortifications erected deeper in the thicket. A commander — Midori— exercising control through restraint and illusion rather than force.


Ishigane listens carefully without interruption.


Michio goes in detail about everything he saw, heard, smelled, felt and thought. Not to give his opinion, but to give unbiased information.


Ishigane’s face does not change, but the room feels smaller. 
When Michio finishes, Ishigane dismisses him with a nod. 
There is no conclusion, only calculation. 


“You did well to return alive. You did better to return with clarity” says Ishigane in the tone of someone who starts to realize something.


He does not issue orders. He does not sound alarms. He ponders.
The words are formal. The weight behind them is not.

A Day That Tries to Be Normal


The next day passes almost as it always does:
Michio trains. He delivers meals, helps at the farms, carries crates, repairs beams, answers greetings and thanks people…
Movement fills the hours. Routine presses itself back into place.


Yet everything feels slightly off, like a path walked too often: dull.


Midori’s calm. The workers’ silence. The fort’s implication.
They surface uninvited, then sink again beneath habit.
Life goes on alright, as everyone does the same thing as always.


By evening, the village lights come on. The day appears to have ended.
Before dawn, a knock comes at Michio’s door. Not loud. Not urgent.
Measured.


He opens his eyes slowly. His body protests immediately — muscles stiff from the previous day’s exertion, joints aching from training layered on top of labor, rest cut short by thoughts that refused to settle. 
Sleep arrived late and it had now been cut short. 


The knock comes again.


Michio sits up, breath shallow for a moment as the weight of the day before returns all at once. Bamboo. Chains. Illusions. Silence.
He dresses quickly, movements practiced but slower than usual. Every stretch pulls at soreness he hasn’t earned time to recover from.


Outside, the village is still dark.


Torches burn low along the paths. The air is cold, sharp with the scent of dew and ash. Hirogawa feels smaller at this hour — stripped of voices, of motion, of the comfort that daylight brings.


Ishigane is here, Michio knows this before he sees him.
The Daimyo stands personally at Michio's door.


“I knew you wouldn’t be ready,” Ishigane says quietly. “But it’s urgent.”


Michio bows, suppressing a yawn that never fully forms.
“I am,” he answers.


Ishigane finally looks at him. His eyes linger — not on Michio’s face, but on the way he stands. The slight stiffness. The controlled breath.


“You didn’t sleep,” Ishigane observes.


“Not much,” Michio admits.


“I apologize,” Ishigane says. “but you understand the situation.”


He turns toward the darkened village.
“The miners were due back before midnight,” he continues. “They know the schedule. They follow it. They have never failed to return.”


Michio says nothing, still taking it all in.


“Nagamizu will accompany you” Ishigane adds, “to make sure no one undesired enters. Or leaves....” he settled heavily.


“Yes, Sir!” says Michio, standing straight despite the aches.


They leave as the horizon begins to pale. Unlike their hopes.
The crane’s calling
The path to the mine cuts through stone and shadow, climbing where the forest thins and the earth hardens. As the village’s embrace loses its grip on the mountain, a drier, rockier path with little bushes siding it. Morning has not yet found this place. Lantern lights bleed light into the atmosphere around the mountain. From this side, it looks like an aura.


Nagamizu walks ahead of Michio, carving the path.
He moved fast, using his staff almost as an extra leg for added stability, breath steady despite the incline. Despite his age. 
This was habit. Discipline. Like the water that carves rivers.


Michio must pick up the pace, even though he is still aching.
Nagamizu slows down, looks over his shoulder and nods at Michio.


Neither of them speaks.


The mine entrance stands before them. No guards. No carts. No voices.


Only heat, emanating quietly, distorting the air in snake-like patterns.
Michio rubbed his eyes, unsure if this was lack of sleep or illusion.


It was very real. Michio realized as he felt a heat wave pulsing out.


Nagamizu stayed at the entrance standing guard, as previously planned. Michio must investigate deeper inside the mines.


The mine's heat radiates outward in faint pulses, warping the air just enough to be felt. Surely a sign of geothermal activity.


Inside, the tunnels are lit by scattered oil lamps, still burning. Tools lie where they were dropped, some impacts imprinted in thin debris.
Tracks end abruptly, as if the ground itself had swallowed those who walked it, but sometimes it appeared deliberate. Not decayed, exploded.


In this chamber there were no signs of fighting. It appeared more and more like sabotage. Blown up tracks, important routes caved in or cut off by massive boulders, which appeared to have been rolled off from smaller galleries above. Dug out strategically from natural formations.


As Michio scans the environment, the odds of the workers having sabotaged their own logistics to prevent the enemy from extracting ore stacked up so high, that they would soon topple over.


Like the hagane bamboo piles.


This floor ends abruptly but the chamber continues. This one was barely carved by people. It was a majestic cave of both rounded rocks and sharp edges. The upper level leading to the outside was separated from the lower area by a depth big enough to cause serious injury on fall.
To Michio’s left, scaffolding circled down along the chamber’s walls, to his right, a crane, made of treated wood and hagane bamboo,


The crane screeched faintly with each swing of the load, held mid-air. It sounded like a bird’s call. Michio saw eyes shimmer under the flickering lights down below. The crane and its load certainly drew some attention.


But not from the workers, who though still alive, aren't enslaved - at least not yet, judging from the previous encounter.
These mine workers are contained inside a cage, and Michio is quick to pick up that the bars react differently to the flickering lights of the tunnels. They aren't made of iron or steel, but of hagane bamboo the previous troops managed to smuggle out.
A voice, sharp and impatient:


“Careful!” The squad leader said "They need to be delivered alive and healthy! And we need to secure the crane before the crate falls!”


These ninjas are as disciplined as the previous but... wearing blue? The squad leader was wearing blue chest armor and large metal pads on the shoulders and legs. The light bouncing off his armor tinted the surroundings in blue. He carried two large shuriken, larger than his head even, about his whole arm's width!


They mean business. It could get ugly in here.

The floor beneath his feet felt warm, not with heat but with presence. The air was clean—too clean after the smoke. Sound behaved strangely here; footsteps did not echo so much as sink, absorbed into the grain of the wood. Somewhere far above—or below—water moved through channels he could not see.
This has to be a dream, Michio thought.
Except dreams did not ache like this. Dreams did not smell of resin and old rain. Dreams did not look back.
He took a step forward. The space did not resist him, but it did not yield either. The walls shifted subtly, not moving so much as deciding where they were willing to be.
Behind him, the entrance he had come through was no longer where he remembered it.
Michio swallowed.
“What… is this place?”
The Sage did not answer immediately. He walked instead—barefoot, unhurried—his steps finding paths Michio could not yet see. As he passed, the bioluminescent veins brightened, responding not to motion, but to recognition.

Michio’s thoughts snagged on the words.
Forget.
Outside, the village burned. Inside, something ancient kept breathing.
He felt it then—not a vision, not a prophecy—but pressure. As if countless moments, decisions, endings pressed gently against him from all sides. None demanding attention. None offering answers.
Just waiting.
Michio closed his eyes.
For the first time since the fire, nothing asked him to run.
And that frightened him more than the flames ever had.

It went all the way in.

Disappearing in Plain Sight
Patrols appeared first, before explanations did.
They moved along routes that had always existed but were rarely used, pairs and trios pacing streets that were usually left to traders, water carriers, and children. Their armor was clean, their steps measured, their presence quiet rather than threatening. They did not stop people without reason. They simply stayed long enough to be noticed.
Notices followed a couple of weeks later. Some were nailed to posts, others hung crookedly from rope or weighted with stones. They all said the same thing, though never quite in the same words.
The Court had prevailed. The challenger was dead. False accounts were treason.
The phrasing shifted depending on where one stood. In one district, the announcement was short and formal, signed with a seal. In another, it was longer, padded with warnings and references to past punishments. A few were replaced within days, new ink bleeding over old lines, as if someone had decided the first version was too vague—or too specific.
Messengers read the proclamations aloud at irregular hours. Sometimes at dawn. Sometimes near dusk. Their voices carried only as far as they needed to, and never invited questions. When asked for clarification, they repeated the same sentence more slowly.
Life adjusted around this.
Markets reopened, but later than usual. Wells were inspected more often, their covers lifted and lowered with practiced efficiency. Guards marked water levels with charcoal lines that were erased and redrawn the next day. No one commented on this. It was treated as routine, even when it clearly was not.
Rumors spread in fragments.
Some said the fight had been brief. Others insisted there had been no fight at all. A few claimed the Court had never been in danger. The details contradicted each other, but the conclusion never changed. Anyone who said otherwise lowered their voice instinctively, even when alone.
A figure passed through these spaces without drawing attention. Sometimes he stood at the edge of a crowd listening. Sometimes he walked when others stopped, or stopped when others walked. People glanced at him and then away again, as if uncertain what they had seen.
Once, someone said the challenger had been taller. Another said younger. Another said there had been more than one.
None of them were correct, and none of them agreed.
Patrols tightened, then loosened, then tightened again. The pattern never settled. It was not a search so much as a reminder. The kind that worked best when it appeared unnecessary.
By the time the statements became law, most people had already begun to act as if they always had been.
San Jiang did not celebrate. It resumed.
After hours of contemplating the thought in front of the mirror,  it was more than clear that the only available escape, for now, was disappearing. Not in person, but from memory.
Michio cut his hair with a borrowed blade that had dulled from too many hands. The strands fell where they stood and were swept away with the ash and dust that never quite settled along the river. He traded his scorched garments for what the slums could spare—patched cloth, river-stiff from salt and sun. Nothing marked him as passing through. Nothing marked him at all.
He took work where it appeared and left when it ended. Carrying crates until his shoulders burned. Clearing silt from channels that refilled by evening. Hauling water that could not be drunk, only moved. The days stacked without ceremony. Mornings began before light. Evenings ended where they began, with hands that shook and a back that would not quite straighten.
No one thanked him. No one asked his name. He was another pair of arms in a place built to consume them. When work ran thin, he waited. When it returned, he bent and lifted again. Food came as bowls passed along, measured and plain. Enough to continue. Never enough to recover.
The riverfront swallowed faces quickly. Patrols passed and did not linger. Notices were read aloud and folded away. Rumors drifted and broke apart on corners where voices lowered. Michio listened when he could and kept his eyes down when he could not.
By the end of the week, he moved without being seen. By the end of the next, he was expected to be where he stood. Survival took root as routine, not as victory. The city did not change for him, and he did not change it in return.

The people who gathered up were watching in disbelief. Michio and the woman just as much. A momentary silence filled every void, every gap, every street and shack. The people were clueless about what's coming next: They just resisted authority.
"Everyone, gather 'round!"  the district leader called.
"Look... Y'all are just as shocked as me, okay?" he paused, letting public murmurs fade. Then proceeded:
"We may be hungry, thirsty, old and beat up - but we know why! For as long as I have been alive, for as long as my parents and grandparents were alive, nothing changed. This is what we always got, this is what we always had. Seigetsu my Ass! This is selfishness, greed, manipulation!"
The crowd roars in agreement. The speech continues:
"You may have noticed: never less than two guards, never more than three. And we see the same guards often. Those who don't get forcefully conscripted or get sent to the arena, do you know who they are? Court members, nobles. The Dynasty is a terror regime sucking us dry since the dawn of this land and we aren't going to take this anymore. We will organize!"
His words echoed loudly, but the river masked the meanings beyond the streets where they were standing. For those who didn't hear, the people made sure to fill them in. As casual talk during work or disguised as a prayer, word spread out.
More and more patrols failed to return. Their shrinking numbers only rang the alarm when it was too late to counter. Then came the conscription raids...
