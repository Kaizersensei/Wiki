Story

Chapter 1 - Hirogawa

Scene 1: Back to the Present

The sound of water pulled Michio back.

Not the imagined rush of memory, but the real thing—the waterfall behind him, steady and loud. The mist clung to his clothes. His legs ached slightly from sitting too long.

He stood, stretched, and exhaled.

The village below was awake now.

Smoke rose from chimneys. Footsteps crossed wooden bridges. Somewhere, metal clinked against stone. Hirogawa moved as it always did—quietly, purposefully, together.

Michio descended the path with practiced ease.

He trained when he could. Sometimes under Nagamizu’s watchful eye, correcting his stance with a word or a tap of the staff. Sometimes alone, repeating movements until his muscles burned and his breath came sharp.

Jump. Roll. Recover.

Again.

When training ended, the day simply continued.

He carried meals from the small restaurant near the square—bowls wrapped in cloth, still warm. The owners always tried to give him extra. He always refused once, then accepted the second time.

At the farms, he lifted crates of produce for the old couple who ran the fields. Their backs were bent. His was not.

“Careful now,” they’d say.

“I’ve got it,” he’d answer, already walking.

Sometimes he helped replace roof beams. Sometimes he held walls steady while others worked. Sometimes he was just another pair of hands passing tools along.

Each task ended the same way.

A nod. A word of thanks.

Something added to the invisible ledger he never meant to keep.

It wasn’t burden.

Not exactly.

But as the sun climbed higher, Michio felt it settle all the same.

By afternoon, a message reached him.

Ishigane wished to see him.

Michio wiped his hands, nodded, and made his way toward the Daimyo’s residence.

Ishigane stood near the open doors, looking out over the village. His posture was as straight as ever, but his brow was drawn low.

“You sent for me?” Michio asked.

Ishigane turned.

“The bamboo workers,” he said without preamble. “They did not return last evening.”

Michio frowned.

“They were due back before dusk,” Ishigane continued. “They are still missing.”

A pause.

“Search parties?” Michio asked.

“Already sent,” Ishigane replied. “No sign. No disturbance. No tools left behind.”

Michio felt a familiar tightening in his chest.

“How many?”

“All ten of them.”

Silence stretched between them.

Ishigane studied him carefully.

“You know these forests,” he said. “You know the paths, the shortcuts. You know when something feels wrong.”

Michio met his gaze.

“Yes.”

Ishigane nodded once.

“Then be careful.”

Coming from Ishigane, this was an unspoken order. Nonetheless, the eerie familiarity of pillars of his life simply disappearing took him over like a rush. He knew he had to be careful lest he end like them—like his parents.

End of Scene 1.

Scene 2: The Bamboo Thicket

The path sloped downward, away from Hirogawa’s terraces and stone paths, following the river as it widened and slowed. The forest thinned first—old trees giving way to younger growth—until the ground flattened and softened underfoot.

Here, the river no longer cut sharply through rock. It spread.

Water pooled between roots and reeds, trapped in shallow wetlands where the soil stayed dark and fertile year-round. Bamboo rose in dense clusters, tall and straight, their leaves whispering softly as the wind passed through them.

Stone gave way to soil. The slope softened. Water slowed, pooled, and spread into shallow wetlands where reeds and young bamboo crowded the banks. The air grew heavier here—cool, damp, alive. Insects hummed. Birds watched from the canopy but did not sing.

Bamboo thrived in this place. So did patience.

Michio moved carefully, feet finding dry ground between puddles. He recognized the terrain immediately. This was where Hirogawa’s bamboo workers harvested—fertile soil, fast growth, easy transport back to the village.

Too easy.

He stopped when the sounds reached him.

Metal scraping wood.

Chains.

Voices—strained, exhausted.

From behind a curtain of leaves, Michio saw them.

Four workers were chained to a thick bamboo pole driven into the mud. Their hands were raw. Their movements mechanical. They worked without pause, cutting and shaping bamboo stalks that gleamed faintly—steel-veined, unnatural.

Hagane bamboo.

Metal-infused. Nearly impervious to melee. Valuable. Dangerous.

The pile beside them was already too large.

Michio crouched lower.

Beyond the workers, movement flickered.

Green-clad figures patrolled the clearing—unarmed, light on their feet. Ninjas. Their posture was relaxed but alert, as if violence were an option they simply hadn’t needed yet.

At the edge of the camp stood their squad leader: a man in a green cap, curly hair spilling out from beneath it. He leaned casually against a bamboo crate, chewing on a blade of grass, eyes half-lidded.

Too casual.

Not in charge—but confident enough to act like he was.

Michio counted routes. Shadows. Wind.

A direct fight would get the workers killed.

He waited.

Cunning Over Force

Michio circled wide, keeping the river’s sound between himself and the camp. He used the wetlands as cover—mud swallowing footsteps, reeds breaking his silhouette.

A tossed stone. A snapped stalk. A ripple where there shouldn’t be one.

One ninja peeled away from the group, moving to investigate.

Michio was already gone.

He drew them off in pairs. Never striking. Never committing. Just enough presence to unsettle—enough absence to confuse.

The squad leader barked orders, irritation creeping into his voice.

“Spread out. Don’t chase shadows.”

That was the opening.

Michio slipped back into the clearing while attention fractured. He worked fast—quiet hands, careful cuts. Chains loosened, then fell.

The first worker nearly collapsed.

“Go,” Michio whispered. “Now. Follow the river. Don’t look back.”

They didn’t argue.

One by one, the four workers vanished into the reeds.

The clearing felt suddenly empty.

Too empty.

Escalation

Silence stretched.

Then voices—new ones.

Another green squad emerged from the treeline, tighter formation, sharper awareness. They took in the scene at a glance: empty chains, disturbed ground, the absence of workers.

The squad leader straightened.

“Trouble,” he muttered.

This time, there was no room to disappear.

Michio stepped out from the shadows.

Steel rang against bamboo as the first clash broke the stillness. The wetlands churned underfoot. Reeds snapped. Water sprayed.

It was fast. Messy. Inevitable.

The air felt wrong—like a breath held too long.

Something else was here.

Not another patrol. Not another mistake.

The wetlands themselves seemed to listen, bamboo leaves freezing mid-whisper as if waiting for permission to move.

When the last guard fell, the air felt wrong—like a breath held too long.

Slow clapping echoed from deeper within the bamboo.

“Well done,” a woman’s voice said, calm and amused. “I was wondering how long it would take before someone noticed the chains.”

The bamboo parted.

She stood there in green—composed, unarmed, eyes sharp with intent. Illusion clung to her presence like perfume, subtle but undeniable.

Midori.

Behind her, deeper within the thicket, Michio glimpsed more structures. More workers. And one figure kept close at her side—separate. Watched.

A private slave.

Michio tightened his grip on his weapon.

An open fight here would cost lives.

And Midori knew it.

The wetlands went quiet.

Not peaceful quiet—listening quiet.

Water rippled where Michio stood, bamboo leaves slowly settling from the earlier clash. His breath was steady, controlled. He didn’t lower his stance.

That was when the air bent.

It wasn’t visible at first. Just a faint distortion, like heat rising from stone—except the wetlands were cold. The bamboo ahead seemed to lean inward, their shadows overlapping where they shouldn’t.

Slow clapping echoed through the thicket.

Measured. Unhurried.

“Well done,” Midori said. “Most people panic much earlier.”

She stepped forward.

She wore green like the others, but cleaner—intentional. No armor. No weapon. Her posture was relaxed, one hand resting idly at her side, the other loosely holding a length of chain.

At the end of that chain stood a worker.

Younger than the others. Barely standing. Eyes unfocused from exhaustion.

Michio’s jaw tightened.

Midori tilted her head slightly, studying him as if he were a puzzle she already half understood.

“You freed four,” she said. “Efficient. Quiet. Compassionate.” A faint smile. “Predictable.”

The wetlands shifted.

For a heartbeat, Michio’s footing wasn’t where he expected. Water reflected bamboo that didn’t exist. Shadows doubled. Tripled.

Illusions.

Not walls—misdirection.

Michio adjusted instantly, lowering his center of gravity. He didn’t attack.

“Let them go,” he said.

Midori laughed softly.

“Oh, I will,” she replied. “Eventually. Survival is a resource. I don’t waste it.”

She snapped her fingers.

Two more green-clad figures appeared—where there had been none a moment before. Whether they had been hidden or created, Michio couldn’t tell.

One lunged.

Michio moved.

He vaulted backward onto a bamboo stalk, using its bend to redirect momentum, springing sideways as a blade passed through the space where his torso had been. His foot connected with the attacker’s wrist—not breaking it, but knocking the arm wide. The second attacker closed in.

The world stuttered.

For half a second, there were three Midoris.

Michio didn’t look at any of them.

He watched the hostage.

The real Midori reacted.

Interesting.

She tightened the chain just enough for the worker to gasp.

Michio froze.

“That hesitation,” Midori said calmly, “that’s your weakness. And your strength.”

She gestured.

The illusions collapsed inward, forcing Michio into a narrow strip of solid ground—mud and water hemming him in. The guards pressed the advantage.

Michio didn’t retreat.

He leapt up.

Using the bamboo itself, he ran—ran—along vertical stalks, shifting his weight with impossible precision. From above, he kicked loose a cluster of Hagane bamboo.

The stalks fell.

Not on the guards.

Between them.

The impact sent a shock through the wet ground, staggering the attackers and breaking their formation. Michio landed beside the chained worker in the same motion, blade already moving.

The chain snapped.

Midori’s eyes widened—just slightly.

The freed worker collapsed. Michio caught him, pulling him clear as a thrown blade cut through where they had been a moment earlier.

Midori exhaled.

“Clever,” she said. “Very clever.”

She stepped back.

The wetlands unwound. The illusions peeled away like mist under sunlight. The remaining guards hesitated—then looked to her.

Midori raised a hand.

“Enough.”

She turned her gaze fully to Michio now.

“You fight like someone who still believes the future can be protected without owning it,” she said. “Hold on to that belief. It makes people… interesting.”

She took another step back.

Bamboo shadows swallowed her.

“Tell Ishigane,” her voice echoed, now everywhere and nowhere, “that the forest remembers who cuts it—and who shapes it.”

Silence returned.

The guards were gone.

Only the wetlands remained.

Michio steadied the rescued worker, scanning the bamboo one last time.

Midori was gone.

But the fort deeper in the thicket still stood.

And now Michio knew:

This wasn’t an accident.

It was a beginning.

End of Scene 2.

Scene 3: The Mine

The path to the mine cuts through stone and shadow, climbing where the forest thins and the earth hardens. Morning has not yet found this place. Lantern light pools against rock, pale and unsteady.

Nagamizu walks ahead of Michio.

He moves without urgency, staff resting lightly in his hand, breath steady despite the incline. The air around him feels cool, damp—water-bound. Familiar.

Neither of them speaks.

The mine entrance gapes open.

No guards. No carts. No voices.

Only heat.

Nagamizu stays here standing guard; Michio must investigate.

The mine’s heat radiates outward in faint pulses, warping the air just enough to be felt. Not fire—not yet—but something restless beneath the stone.

Inside, the tunnels are lit by scattered crystal lamps, still burning. Tools lie where they were dropped. Tracks end abruptly, as if the ground itself had swallowed those who walked it.

And deeper still—movement.

But not from the workers, who though still alive, aren’t enslaved—at least not yet, judging from the previous encounter.

These mine workers are contained inside a cage, and Michio is quick to pick up that they react differently to the flickering lights of the tunnels. They aren’t made of iron or steel, but of Hagane bamboo the previous troops managed to smuggle out.

A voice, sharp and impatient:

“Careful!” The squad leader said, “They need to be delivered alive and healthy!”

These ninjas are as disciplined as the previous but—wearing blue? The squad leader wears a blue chest armor and large protective pads on the shoulders and legs. He carries two large shuriken, larger than his head even, about his whole arm’s width.

They mean business. It could get ugly in here.

From where Michio is standing, the mine opens up into a wider room which is some kind of loading area. Minecart tracks start and end here. Some waiting to be unloaded, others empty and ready to return. Michio stands at a higher point than the adversary. There is a crane nearby, holding a container full of ore.

Michio rolls a stone over the ledge, so that it falls near the place where the container is hanging. It echoes loudly and reverbs through the mine.

“Who’s there?” asks the squad leader. Only his echoes answered.

Michio rolls another stone.

Suspicious, the leader gestures for the foot soldiers to move out. One of them throws a shuriken as a warning.

“Interesting, the small fry also have small weapons,” Michio thought, as the shuriken whizzed by.

But they were smart enough to stay away from the dangling crate. They started climbing the scaffolding and taking position. The squad leader stood by the cage, containing three terrified workers.

The grunts were starting to make progress in finding sniping positions to end the yet undiscovered Michio with one silent, swift shuriken. Michio had to think fast.

He did not rush them.

Killing them here would be easy—too easy—and it would turn the cage into a death sentence for the workers below. Instead, he shifted higher along the scaffolding, letting his silhouette appear just long enough to draw attention.

A shuriken tore through the space he had occupied an instant before.

“Up there!” someone shouted.

Michio ran.

Wood groaned beneath his feet as he sprinted along the beams, leaping gaps without hesitation. The ninjas followed, spreading out, trusting their numbers and angles. That was the mistake.

The first clash came fast.

A guard lunged. Michio pivoted, using the narrow beam to his advantage—one step, one shove, just enough force. The man lost footing and fell hard into the mud below, alive but unmoving.

The squad leader barked an order.

Two more advanced together.

Michio waited until they committed, then dropped low, sweeping one leg and slamming his shoulder into the other. Steel rang. Bodies hit wood. One rolled away clutching his arm. The other did not rise.

The leader snarled and hurled one of his massive shuriken. It carved through a support beam, burying itself deep in stone.

The scaffolding shuddered.

That was when Michio saw it.

The crane.

The container of ore swayed gently above the chamber floor, held by thick chains—heavy enough to crush, controlled enough to aim.

Michio sprinted.

He vaulted the last gap as another shuriken tore past his shoulder, skidding along the beam where his head had been. He landed beside the crane controls, hands already moving.

The squad leader realized too late.

“Don’t—!”

Michio released the brake.

The container dropped. The noise of rolling ropes was almost deafening.

It smashed through the second wave as they advanced, scattering them like debris, sending bodies into walls, into water, into silence. The chamber filled with echoes and steam.

The workers were far away enough to be spared by the crash and debris blast, but dust filled the huge cave room where the logistics were taking place. They were okay—coughing from the dust—but still okay.

When the dust finally settled, the mine was still.

The squad leader stood alone now, weapon raised, breathing hard. His confidence was gone—replaced by fury.

The leader throws his two big shuriken in a crossing motion, aiming to trap Michio between both.

This time, Michio did not retreat. He dodged them and stood his ground.

What he didn’t expect was that the shuriken would fly back to the hands of their owner. Some kind of magnetism? Michio could not fully understand, as he barely dodged the returning weapons with a backflip to a ducking stance.

Defeating him would require him to let go of his weapons, and he’s well-armored. And his weapons return when thrown.

They met on the scaffolding, beam against beam. The leader fought brutally, wide arcs, relying on distance and momentum, hopping around to keep ideal distance.

Michio let him overextend, deflected or dodged, stayed close. Still no opening.

Maybe Michio can trick him into missing a throw or a catch. He doesn’t quite know, as he studies the opponent with each exchange.

He lets the leader throw both his shuriken, predicting their motion and most importantly—their return.

A pull of the wrist. A chokehold from behind.

The restrained leader panics, thrashing violently, trying to shake Michio loose. Elbows strike ribs. Armor scrapes skin. Michio absorbs it, feet planted, waiting.

The shuriken return.

Michio releases his grip at the last possible instant and drives his heel forward.

The leader stumbles into the path of his own weapons.

The impact is final.

Both shuriken punch clean through armor and body, carrying him backward until the stone above the tunnel entrance stops him. Metal bites rock. Blood runs down the arch in slow, uneven lines.

Silence follows.

Michio catches himself against the railing, breath sharp, arms trembling—not from effort, but restraint. He had wanted it to end differently. Less… red.

He moves immediately.

The cage.

The Hagane bamboo bars do not bend. Do not crack. No keys are found on the fallen soldiers.

One of the workers, voice hoarse but steady, speaks through the bars.

“Even Hagane’ll go down with a good ol’ bang. There are explosives in the lower levels. Mining charges. It’s the only way.”

The workers recoil at the word.

Then exchange looks.

Then nod.

They accept the risk.

Descent — The Search for Explosives

The mine did not descend cleanly.

The main tunnel split into older veins carved at different times, different urgencies. Some passages were reinforced with timber and iron brackets. Others were bare stone, scarred by tool marks that spiraled inward like hesitation frozen in rock. Heat bled unevenly through the walls—faint in some corridors, oppressive in others—a pulse rather than a gradient.

Michio followed it instinctively.

Not blindly. Carefully.

Minecart tracks branched and rejoined like veins. Some carts sat idle, half-filled with ore. Others were stacked, derailed, or overturned, wheels still warm to the touch. The tracks themselves told stories: heavy loads dragged downhill, empty carts rushed back up. This was not a place designed for elegance—only for extraction.

He moved with intent.

Where the heat increased, the stone cracked more often. Water pooled where the rock sagged inward, collecting beneath stalactites that dripped in slow, uneven rhythms. Each drop echoed too loudly. Each echo risked attention.

The first patrol appeared without warning.

Blue-clad figures moved between tunnels, looser now in formation, numbers greater than before. Some guarded dead ends. Others lingered near resource piles—bundles of Hagane bamboo, crates of ore, discarded tools. Their placement was inefficient, almost careless, as if the mine itself had been doing the guarding for them.

Michio did not engage.

He used the minecarts instead.

A light shove sent an empty cart rolling down a side track, its wheels screaming against bent iron. Voices followed the sound. Shadows moved. Michio slipped the opposite way, scaling a support beam as a pair of ninjas rushed past below.

Further down, the tunnels narrowed.

Here, the air grew thick.

Heat pressed in waves, carrying with it the sharp, acrid scent of mineral powder and old oil. The walls were scorched—not burned, but stressed. This was where controlled destruction had once been necessary.

The storage chamber made sense the moment he saw it.

It sat low and deep, cut into the rock where collapse would travel upward instead of outward. Thick stone walls. Reinforced ceiling. A single, narrow access tunnel designed to funnel pressure.

Crates were stacked in ordered rows.

Each bore the same faded markings.

Mining charges.

Michio exhaled slowly.

Another patrol swept through the chamber as he crouched behind a broken cart, their boots kicking loose gravel into the heat-hazed air. One laughed. Another complained about the temperature. None of them lingered.

When they were gone, Michio moved.

He secured the charges carefully, testing weight, checking fuses—old work, but reliable. The kind of tools meant to reshape mountains, not people.

The mine shifted.

A low groan rolled through the stone as something deeper settled. Pebbles rained from the ceiling. A stalactite cracked and shattered against the floor, sending fragments skidding across the track.

Michio froze.

Then kept going.

By the time he turned back toward the workers’ cage, the mine felt different.

Awake.

Time to rush back to the workers. Nagamizu moved further in, after making sure no one would go through the narrow—and only—exit. He gestures at Michio to throw the explosives satchel and keep moving. Four fingers raised, four workers left. Nagamizu binds the explosives under the workers’ direction. Michio moves towards the entrance of the explosives depot, waiting until the explosion’s echo reaches the lower levels.

BANG!

As loud as it was for Michio, it was surely the same for the enemies, who panicked in fear of the mine collapsing. The explosion sent vibrations through all layers of soil and mineral. Pebbles fell from the ceiling and a dust cloud was blown under high pressure through the lower tunnels, breaking stalactites and shaking the flimsy scaffoldings and rail tracks, throwing some enemies off balance and plunging them into injury or death.

The remainder burst out of the explosives depot, disorganized and in varying numbers, never in a pattern. Panic was obvious and spreading like wildfire.

Michio dispatched them silently and quickly in the same order they came out.

As Michio enters the room, he realizes one enemy ninja stood back. He holds a torch.

“Not a step closer, fire-head! Or I’ll make you, me, EVERYONE here glow ember red... ha.. hahahaha!” screams the soldier.

Michio’s pupils widen; he feels a cold sweat drop roll down his spine.

“You... you wouldn’t do it! Don’t you have someone you love? Someone you want to return to?” asks Michio, composed but in tension. If the mine blows up, the adjacent village and the Infinitree would get collateral damage of unpredictable dimensions. He knew he had to think fast, and act faster.

“HAHAHAHA! FOOL!” The soldier’s laughter sends another chill down Michio’s spine. Why would a soldier risk compromising the whole operation? Michio tries to formulate a sentence but is promptly interrupted:

“Those who join the Ninth Wave rarely have anything to call their own. All taken or ravaged by the likes of you, waging war, pillaging our settlements!”

“We... what?” Michio was incredulous. As far as there are records, Hirogawa was never involved in a single war, skirmish or covert op after its founding. Michio tries to say something but the soldier is already laying the torch down on the explosives pile.

“So long... this world didn’t make sense anyway...” says the soldier, a mere second before dropping his torch.

A water barrage floods the room. A focused water stream extinguishes the torch. The warrior is frozen in disbelief.

“Let’s see you burn the wets, then!” says Nagamizu, calmly as he appears in the room. “Go, and tell your boss we are not afraid.”

The soldier stumbles, falls on his behind, then promptly gets up and runs deeper into the mine.

“Now we are sure of their location!” says Nagamizu. “Michio, lead on!”

End of Explosives Search Section.

Attention in the Deep

A lone soldier stumbles into the deeper chamber.

His armor is scorched. His breath is ragged. He does not speak.

He drops to one knee before the one waiting beyond, presses a fist to the ground, and bows his head.

He traces shapes into the dust with trembling fingers.

Water.

Explosions.

An old man.

Silence stretches.

A single voice answers, bright with anticipation.

Aoi: Water? This is going to be fun!

Something deeper in the mine had noticed.

A massive egg, scaled and veined with ember-light, half-buried in fractured rock. Heat rolls off it in waves. The stone around it is cracked, blackened—ancient.

A dragon egg.

Aoi stands before it.

She is young—younger than Michio expected—clad in ice-blue and white, with short hair in snow whites and ice blues. Her spear is planted into the ground beside her, its shaft rimed with frost where it touches stone.

Her soldiers move nervously around the chamber, keeping distance from the egg.

Nagamizu exhales.

“So that is what they buried here,” he says quietly.

Aoi turns.

Her eyes lock onto him immediately.

Recognition flashes—then irritation.

“Of course they sent you,” she snaps. “The old man who thinks he understands time because he refuses to use it.”

Nagamizu plants his staff.

“You should not be here,” he replies. “This creature is not yours.”

Aoi laughs, sharp and humorless.

“Nothing here belongs to Hirogawa,” she says. “Not anymore.”

The egg cracks.

A sound like splitting glass echoes through the chamber.

The dragon hatches as the first clash begins.

Ice meets water.

Aoi lunges, spear flashing. Nagamizu counters instinctively, drawing moisture from the air, the stone, the very breath between them. Water coils, intercepts—and freezes.

A mistake.

The spear drinks it in.

Ice surges along the weapon, blooming outward, stronger than before. Aoi grins.

“Thank you,” she says.

The spear pierces Nagamizu’s chest.

The dragon screams.

Fire erupts.

Nagamizu falls without a sound.

For a moment, the world stops.

Then Aoi moves.

She vaults onto the hatchling as flames burst uncontrolled from its jaws, tearing through the chamber, racing up the tunnels. She doesn’t look back.

“To hell with this place,” she shouts over the roar.

The mine burns.

The fire spreads.

By the time Michio reaches the surface, Hirogawa is already alight.

End of Scene 3.

Scene 4: The Fire Runs Faster Than Men

Smoke claws at the sky.

Flames lick across rooftops, racing faster than the wind should allow. Embers drift like falling stars, settling into thatch, cloth, memory. The familiar paths of Hirogawa are broken—bridges half-collapsed, alleys choked with burning debris, the air thick with heat and ash.

Michio stumbles out of the mine entrance, coughing, eyes stinging. The roar behind him swells—fire fed by something deeper than wood.

“No”—he breathes.

There are no screams.

That is what strikes him first.

No villagers running. No bodies. No cries for help.

Only fire.

A low bark cuts through the chaos.

Michio turns.

A dog stands at the edge of the smoke—broad-shouldered, scarred, eyes sharp and steady despite the flames reflecting in them. Betto. Ishigane’s envoy. He does not approach.

He looks back over his shoulder.

Then runs.

Michio hesitates only a moment before following.

Betto does not take the main paths.

He darts through gaps Michio barely fits through, across fallen beams still burning at the edges, under collapsing eaves. When Betto stops, Michio stops. When Betto lowers himself, Michio knows to duck. When Betto refuses a route—hackles raised, nose low—Michio does not question it.

Enemy patrols move through the smoke, silhouettes in blue and white cutting through flame-lit haze. Betto veers away before they come into view, forcing Michio onto rooftops, into narrow vertical climbs, into routes that demand commitment.

The distance between them stretches.

Betto moves ahead. Vanishes. Reappears.

Each disappearance tightens something in Michio’s chest.

A crash.

A house gives way ahead—its roof collapsing inward, fire bursting through the windows in a violent bloom. From within comes a sharp, panicked yelp.

Michio doesn’t think.

He runs.

The heat is immediate, overwhelming. Smoke blinds him as he dives through a broken doorway. Inside, beams crack and fall. Fire crawls along the walls.

Kyo is trapped beneath a fallen plank, fur singed, teeth bared in fear.

“Easy,” Michio says, voice hoarse.

He wedges his shoulder beneath the beam and lifts. Pain screams through his muscles, but the wood shifts just enough. Kyo scrambles free, seeking shelter between Michio’s legs. Michio loosens up his gi top, picks him up and nests him inside the garment. He tightens it back enough so that the pup won’t slip out and leaves the house roughly a minute before the walls collapse.

The ceiling collapses seconds later.

Michio bursts back into the street, rolling clear as sparks rain down around him.

Kyo sounds uneasy, but coils up and calms down from Michio’s body heat, like a baby kangaroo in the mother’s purse.

Michio keeps following Betto’s trail.

The Infinitree rises ahead, its silhouette cutting through smoke and fire like a promise and a warning at once.

Enemy figures converge from multiple paths now, drawn by the blaze, by orders shouted through the chaos. Fire itself becomes an obstacle—walls of flame forcing detours, burning debris collapsing into sudden dead ends.

Betto appears again, barking sharply, then takes a high route along the stone terraces.

Michio follows, breath burning, legs screaming, the weight of everything pressing in at once.

The closer he gets to the tree, the more wrong the fire feels.

Too focused.

Too deliberate.

As Michio reaches the outer roots of the Infinitree, the ground trembles—not from collapse, but from something moving beneath the heat.

He looks back once.

Hirogawa burns.

And still—no bodies.

Only absence.

Betto stops at the door to the Sage’s and finally turns to face him, standing firm despite the heat.

For the first time since the fire began, Michio slows.

The Sage signals Michio to come in and hurry up.

“I was afraid you’d never come. That... would be even more catastrophic than what’s about to come. Get in, brace tight!” says the Sage.

Of all that could happen next, being granted entrance to the Sage’s was not in Michio’s bingo card.

Whatever comes next—

It was not meant to end here.

End of Scene 4.
